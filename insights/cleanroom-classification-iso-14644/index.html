Cleanroom Classification to ISO 14644-1: Sampling, Grades and Requalification for Pharma

Cleanroom classification looks like a particle-counting exercise, but most inspection findings come from decisions made before the counter is switched on: the wrong occupancy state, sampling locations copied from an old layout, or an ISO class reported as if it were an EU GMP grade. This guide walks through what ISO 14644-1:2015 actually requires and how it fits with EU GMP Annex 1.

By Mohammad Awawdeh · Published and reviewed 23 November 2026 · 7 min read

What ISO 14644-1 classifies, and what it does not

ISO 14644-1 classifies air cleanliness by the concentration of airborne particles only. It defines nine classes, from ISO Class 1 to ISO Class 9, across particle sizes from 0.1 µm to 5 µm, using the relationship Cn = 10N × (0.1/D)2.08, where N is the class number and D the particle size in µm. In practice, pharmaceutical facilities work with ISO 5, 7 and 8, most often at the 0.5 µm and 5.0 µm thresholds.

The standard says nothing about microbial contamination, surface cleanliness, airflow pattern or product protection. Those belong to the wider qualification described in HVAC validation and to the site's contamination control strategy. Classification is a point-in-time demonstration that a room meets a class; routine environmental monitoring is the ongoing evidence that it keeps doing so. Treating one as a substitute for the other is a common gap.

Fix the occupancy state and grade before you count

ISO 14644-1 recognises three occupancy states: as-built (installation complete, no equipment running), at-rest (equipment installed and operating, no personnel present) and operational (the room functioning in its defined manner with the specified number of personnel). The protocol must state which state each test represents, because the same room carries different limits in each.

EU GMP Annex 1 (2022) maps its grades onto ISO classes as follows for maximum airborne particle concentration:

Classification at rest and in operation are separate tests. A Grade B room that passes at rest has not demonstrated anything about operational performance, and a report that does not state the occupancy state cannot support either claim.

Sampling locations: the 2015 table replaced the square-root rule

The 2015 revision removed the old √A rule for the number of locations. ISO 14644-1 Annex A now gives a minimum number of sampling locations against the cleanroom area, derived so that at least 90% of the area can be shown, with 95% confidence, not to exceed the class limit. As examples, a 10 m² room needs at least 5 locations, a 50 m² room at least 10 and a 1,000 m² area at least 27; above 1,000 m² the number scales with area.

The room is then divided into that number of sections of roughly equal area, with at least one location in each, positioned at the level of work activity. Locations required by the standard are a minimum: add locations at critical points such as filling zones, open product or material transfers where the risk assessment calls for them, and record the rationale on a drawing.

Because the location count is now statistically derived, the 2015 version also dropped the 95% upper confidence limit calculation. Instead, each individual location must meet the class limit. A single failing location is a failing classification, which must be investigated rather than averaged away.

Sample volume, counters and the 5.0 µm problem

The minimum single sample volume per location is Vs = (20 / Cn,m) × 1,000 litres, where Cn,m is the class limit for the largest particle size being considered. The volume must be at least 2 litres and the sampling time at least one minute. This formula ensures that at least 20 particles would be counted if the concentration were exactly at the limit.

That is why the 2015 revision stopped specifying a 5.0 µm limit for ISO 5. At the old limit of 29 particles per m³, the formula requires roughly 690 litres per location, and large-particle sampling losses make the result hard to defend. Where 5.0 µm data is still needed, ISO 14644-1 offers the macroparticle (M descriptor) approach.

Particle counters should be calibrated to ISO 21501-4, with the flow rate, sampling tube length and calibration status recorded for each run. Printouts or exported raw data should be retained and reviewed as GMP records, so that the classification report can be traced to the original counts.

Requalification intervals and change triggers

ISO 14644-2:2015 replaced fixed reclassification intervals with a risk-based monitoring plan. That plan defines how particle data is collected between classifications and supports the interval chosen. For EU GMP facilities, Annex 1 sets the maximum interval: requalification of Grade A and B areas at least every six months, and Grade C and D areas at least every twelve months.

Significant changes should also trigger an impact assessment and, where needed, requalification before the room returns to use. Typical triggers are HVAC modifications, filter replacement, layout or equipment changes, changes in operational occupancy, and repeated monitoring excursions. Classification results should be reviewed alongside differential pressure, airflow and monitoring trends instead of being filed as a standalone certificate.

Gaps that commonly appear in classification packages

  • Only at-rest results reported, but used to justify operational use.
  • Sampling locations carried over from a previous protocol after the layout changed.
  • No drawing or rationale linking locations to area sections and critical points.
  • Routine monitoring alert and action limits confused with classification limits.
  • Counter calibration, flow rate or sample volume not recorded, or raw data missing.
  • Failing locations re-tested without a documented investigation.

Each of these can be prevented at protocol stage, which is where review effort pays back most.

A defensible classification path

  1. 1Define grade, ISO class and occupancy state
  2. 2Derive locations and sample volumes
  3. 3Execute with calibrated counters and raw data
  4. 4Report per location and set the requalification interval

Frequently asked questions

Is an EU GMP Grade the same as an ISO class?

No. The ISO class describes airborne particle concentration only. An EU GMP grade adds the occupancy state, the 5.0 µm limits where specified, microbial limits and the monitoring expectations of Annex 1. Grade B, for example, corresponds to ISO 5 at rest but ISO 7 in operation.

Do we still need to calculate the 95% upper confidence limit?

Not under ISO 14644-1:2015. The revision replaced the square-root location formula and the 95% UCL calculation with a statistically derived location table. Instead, every individual sampling location must meet the class limit.

How often must a pharmaceutical cleanroom be reclassified?

ISO 14644-2 makes the interval a risk-based decision supported by a monitoring plan. EU GMP Annex 1 sets a maximum: requalification of Grade A and B areas at least every six months and Grade C and D areas at least every twelve months, plus requalification after significant changes.
