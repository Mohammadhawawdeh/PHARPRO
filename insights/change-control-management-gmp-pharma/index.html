GMP Change Control Management: Classifying, Assessing and Closing Changes

Change control turns a planned modification to a facility, system or process into a documented, risk-assessed decision instead of an undocumented drift from the validated state. Treating it as a signature step rather than a cross-functional risk assessment is a recurring theme in FDA and EU GMP inspection findings.

By Mohammad Awawdeh · Published and reviewed 13 October 2026

Draw the line between change control and CAPA

Change control manages planned, intentional modifications - to facilities, utilities, equipment, computerised systems, processes, specifications, materials or suppliers - before they are implemented. CAPA responds to something that has already gone wrong. A deviation that triggers a corrective action which itself alters a validated state should hand off into a change control record for that modification, rather than closing the CAPA with an unassessed change buried inside the action plan.

Every GxP-impacting modification needs its own unique, traceable change record, including changes initiated by suppliers, software vendors or outsourced facilities, and changes that look purely administrative, such as a label text revision or a role reassignment in a computerised system - categories reviewers routinely underestimate.

Classify and risk-assess before anyone touches anything

Classification - commonly like-for-like, minor, major or critical - should be assigned through a documented, risk-based procedure rather than by whoever raised the request, because classification determines how deep the impact assessment, approval chain and verification burden need to go.

The impact assessment must be cross-functional: QA, validation, engineering, regulatory affairs, supply chain and, where relevant, the receiving site. It should explicitly address whether the change affects requalification or revalidation, method or cleaning validation, master documents, training, supplier qualification status, and any product already on the market under an existing regulatory filing.

Scope the implementation and verification plan up front

Approve the change before execution begins, with a defined implementation plan: who performs each qualification or verification step, which documents need updating - URS, risk assessments, SOPs, master batch or production records, specifications, drawings - and what training is required before go-live.

Decide the requalification or revalidation scope as part of approval, not as an afterthought once work is already underway. A change that looks like-for-like on paper but alters a critical parameter, supplier or software version still needs objective evidence that the validated state is preserved.

Close the record with evidence, not a signature

A change control record is not closed when implementation finishes; it is closed when verification and effectiveness evidence confirm the intended outcome was achieved without unintended consequences, often after a defined monitoring period.

Emergency and temporary changes need the same rigour on a compressed timeline: a documented risk assessment before implementation, a target date to make the change permanent or reverse it, and periodic trending of how often emergency change is used - a high emergency-change rate is itself a quality-system weakness that inspectors look for.

A defensible execution path

  1. 1Classify and risk-assess the change
  2. 2Run the cross-functional impact assessment
  3. 3Implement with the agreed requalification scope
  4. 4Verify effectiveness and close with evidence

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between change control and CAPA?

Change control manages planned, proactive modifications to a validated or controlled state. CAPA addresses something that has already gone wrong. A corrective action that itself changes a validated process, system or document should be executed and documented through change control, not inside the CAPA record alone.

Does every change need full revalidation?

No. The verification scope should be proportionate to the documented risk and classification of the change. A like-for-like replacement may need only confirmation testing, while a change to a critical process parameter, software version or supplier typically needs a defined requalification or revalidation scope agreed before implementation.

How should emergency or temporary changes be handled?

Through the same change control process on a compressed timeline: a documented risk assessment before implementation, a target date to make the change permanent or reverse it, and periodic review of emergency-change frequency, which inspectors treat as an indicator of quality-system control.
